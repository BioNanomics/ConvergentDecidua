# AI Phase Plan for Convergent Regulatory Changes in Spontaneous Decidualization

## Executive summary

The best first implementation phase is **not** a fully general three-species foundation-model project. The public data landscape is asymmetric: humans have rich cycle-resolved transcriptomic and chromatin data, mice have strong decidualization atlases and are the best immediate non-spontaneous-decidu\-alization outgroup, bats now have pregnancy-stage maternal-fetal interface data, and the Egyptian spiny mouse still appears to have much thinner public omics coverage centered on genome and transcriptome references rather than uterus-specific single-cell or chromatin assays. Because spontaneous decidualization is reported in humans, some bats, and the spiny mouse, while ordinary laboratory mice decidualize only after embryo-driven signaling, the most actionable phase is a **human-first, mouse-anchored, bat-validation, spiny-mouse-sequence-first** design. citeturn24search1turn24search13turn24search16turn8search11turn14view1turn20search0turn20search13

For implementation, make **scRNA-seq / snRNA-seq the backbone**, use **bulk RNA-seq** to densify time axes and perturbation signals, add **ATAC-seq and receptor cistromes** as causal priors where available, and treat **spatial data** mainly as a validation layer. On the AI side, the highest-value stack is: **scGPT or scvi-tools/scANVI for harmonization and label transfer**, **Geneformer for human-centric regulator prioritization**, **GENIE3 as the expression-only GRN baseline**, **LINGER only where paired RNA+ATAC exists or pseudo-multiome is credible**, and **Enformer/Borzoi as the reproducible open sequence baselines**, with **AlphaGenome** as an optional frontier model if API access and license constraints fit the project. citeturn29search2turn29search21turn25search6turn25search3turn26search4turn26search10turn27search0turn47search0turn47search2

Most public source data for this phase are housed in entity["organization","NCBI","us biomedical archive"] archives such as GEO and SRA, or in entity["organization","EMBL-EBI","uk bioinformatics institute"] resources such as ArrayExpress and BioStudies. citeturn30view0turn38view0turn36search11turn37search1turn46search4

## Model and library stack

The highest-confidence stack is to use a **two-layer AI design**. Layer one learns cross-species cell states and candidate regulators from expression and, where available, chromatin. Layer two scores orthologous or syntenic regulatory sequences for their predicted impact on accessibility and transcription. The key practical point is that data asymmetry argues for **fine-tuning and transfer**, not from-scratch pretraining. Human-only models such as Geneformer are especially useful for ranking human decidual regulators and then projecting that shortlist into bat and spiny ortholog space; models like scGPT and scvi-tools are better suited to integration and cross-dataset harmonization. citeturn29search2turn29search21turn47search0turn47search2

## Ortholog strategy

Use a **three-tier ortholog map**, not a single collapsed table. Tier one is the **strict one-to-one backbone**, which should drive cross-species integration, cell-state transfer, and the primary convergence tests. Tier two is a **permissive orthogroup layer** for genes that are one-to-many or many-to-many in bats or spiny mouse. Tier three is a **regulatory-synteny layer** for elements, because element-level conservation often fails long before gene-level orthology becomes ambiguous. This separation matters because Ensembl explicitly distinguishes one-to-one, one-to-many, and many-to-many orthologues, and also provides orthology quality controls such as gene-order conservation and whole-genome-alignment scores. citeturn27search6turn27search10

The concrete mapping rule should be: **human as anchor species**, then map to mouse and bat through Ensembl Compara/BioMart where possible, and use g:Orth as a batch identifier-conversion and cross-check layer because it is built from Ensembl ortholog mappings. For the spiny mouse, which is the least mature of the focal species in public annotation terms, use OrthoDB as the broad-coverage fallback and then validate critical mappings with reciprocal best hits against the latest assembly and transcriptome. For primary discovery, exclude unresolved paralogs from the strict backbone and analyze them only in a secondary family-aware pass. citeturn27search2turn27search3turn28search0turn28search1

At the regulatory level, compare **orthologous genes directly** but compare **candidate regulatory elements by synteny, motif grammar, and TE family**, not just by exact sequence alignment. That is especially important here because both comparative uterine transcriptomics and human cyclical chromatin studies point to heavy transposable-element involvement in endometrial regulatory remodeling, making TE-aware convergence tests more informative than strict basewise conservation alone. citeturn11search13turn12search3turn40search7

## Implementation workflow

Start with a **processed-matrix-first ingestion strategy**. Pull matrices and metadata first from GEO, SRA-derived processed files, and ArrayExpress/BioStudies, and defer raw FASTQ remapping to datasets that are chromatin-based, explicitly perturbational, or missing reliable processed outputs. For 10x-style single-cell data, either use the provided processed matrices or reprocess with Cell Ranger; its current documentation states that Cell Ranger uses the STAR aligner under the hood. For bulk RNA-seq, use Salmon for fast transcript quantification unless you specifically need genome-coordinate alignments for splice-aware inspection or track generation. citeturn47search1turn47search13turn47search3turn47search7

For QC, I would use these **starting parameters**. For scRNA/snRNA: keep cells with at least 500 genes detected in human and mouse, relax to 300 for low-depth bat or pilot spiny analyses, remove cells above 15% mitochondrial reads in human and mouse and above 20% only in exploratory non-model analyses, and remove doublets with an expected rate of 5–10%. Build pseudobulk profiles only from donor × stage × cell-type bins with at least 20 cells. Keep raw counts in `h5ad` and write one donor-harmonized metadata table as the system of record.

Then build the integration in two passes. Pass one is a **species-specific atlas pass** using Scanpy plus scvi-tools to create high-confidence cell labels for human and mouse separately. Pass two is a **cross-species reduced-gene pass** using the strict one-to-one ortholog backbone and a stromal-focused state ontology: stromal fibroblast, pre-decidual, decidual, senescent-decidual, endothelial/perivascular, immune, and glandular epithelial. Use scANVI or a similar semi-supervised model for label transfer, and deliberately hold out one species at a time to test whether the latent space generalizes. citeturn47search2turn47search0turn47search6turn8search11turn38view0turn43view0

For model fitting, keep the phase narrow. Fine-tune scGPT on ortholog-reduced stromal and neighboring cell states across human, mouse, and bat. Run Geneformer primarily on the human stromal subset to produce a ranked list of candidate upstream regulators under decidual progression and senescence branching. Use ranked outputs rather than end-to-end classifier predictions as the handoff into downstream GRN and sequence analyses. Because Geneformer is human-pretrained, treat its cross-species value as **shortlist generation through ortholog projection**, not as a literal multi-species foundation model. citeturn29search2turn29search21turn25search1

For GRN inference, run **GENIE3 first** on stromal-cell pseudobulk and time-ordered human and mouse series, then intersect its edges with human ATAC and receptor-cistrome support. Use LINGER only when you can credibly construct matched RNA and chromatin representations for the same cell states; otherwise it should remain a secondary method. The output of this layer should be a TF–target table and, where possible, a TF–regulatory-element–target table for each species, plus a merged comparison table keyed by strict orthologs. citeturn25search3turn25search6turn30view0turn31view0turn10view0

For sequence-based regulatory prediction, take the top 200–1,000 candidate elements from the GRN layer and score them with Enformer or Borzoi using human, bat, spiny-mouse, and mouse sequences in matched syntenic windows. Use three perturbation modes: **native scoring**, **motif ablation**, and **sequence swap**. AlphaGenome should only be brought in at the end to rescore the top shortlist if preview API access is acceptable. This phase should explicitly test whether candidate elements near orthologous genes show concordant predicted effects in humans and bats, and then in spiny mouse where synteny is clear, while diverging in the mouse outgroup. citeturn26search4turn26search10turn27search0

For convergence testing, use a layered design. Test convergence at the **gene level** by overlap and rank aggregation of differential expression and regulator scores. Test convergence at the **network level** by overlap of regulons and TF-target edges. Test convergence at the **element level** by shared predicted sequence effects, shared motif turnover, and TE-family enrichment. Finally, add a **phylogenetic comparative layer** only after expanding the comparison set beyond the focal trio, because strong claims about convergence are much more credible with additional mammalian outgroups such as rabbit, opossum, and non-menstruating rodents. citeturn12search17turn24search1turn24search13

## Evaluation and deliverables

The evaluation should be built around five metrics families. For **cross-species transfer**, report macro-F1, balanced accuracy, and confusion matrices for hierarchical cell labels under leave-one-species-out transfer. For **trajectory fidelity**, compare pseudotime orderings to known cycle or experimental order using Spearman correlation and donor-aware concordance. For **GRN quality**, use AUPR or AUROC against edges supported by ATAC, ChIP/CUT&RUN, and literature-backed decidual regulators. For **sequence scoring**, report correlation between predicted and observed accessibility or expression shifts, plus AUROC for held-out positive regulatory elements. For **convergence**, report empirical permutation p-values, effect sizes, and FDR-adjusted q-values for shared genes, regulons, motifs, and TE families. citeturn25search3turn25search6turn26search4turn26search10turn11search13turn40search7

A strong validation plan should include at least three orthogonal checks. First, test whether candidate regulators predict the known effects in human and mouse perturbation datasets such as the TRIM28 series. Second, confirm that top predicted regulators are supported by motif accessibility or receptor cistrome evidence in human endometrium. Third, confirm that top sequence-level candidates remain prioritized when using both an open model baseline and, if available, AlphaGenome. If you can add one wet-lab follow-on after this computational phase, the best choices are CRISPRi reporter assays or targeted enhancer-reporter swaps in human stromal cells. citeturn42view0turn10view0turn31view0turn27search0

The deliverables should be concrete and machine-usable. I would require: a `dataset_registry.csv`; an `ortholog_backbone.parquet`; species-specific and integrated `h5ad` objects; an `h5mu` object if any paired multiome is assembled; ranked GRN edge tables in `parquet`; candidate regulatory elements in `BED`; sequence-model scores in `parquet`; browser tracks in `bigWig`; motif and TE enrichment tables in `tsv`; and a simple interactive atlas built from `h5ad` or `zarr`. The central ranked output should be a **candidate short list** with columns for ortholog status, cell-state specificity, GRN support, chromatin support, sequence-model score, TE annotation, convergence score, and validation priority.

## Infrastructure and governance

For compute, a realistic **Phase 1** estimate is modest if you stay matrix-first: roughly **0.5–1.5 TB** of storage for processed objects and intermediate artifacts. If you decide to reprocess a large fraction of raw FASTQs, plan more conservatively for **5–10 TB**. For training and fine-tuning, a single **40 GB GPU** is enough for most scGPT or scvi-tools jobs on stromal subsets; two 40–80 GB GPUs are more comfortable if you keep broad multi-species cell sets in memory or run repeated sequence-model rescoring. CPU needs are moderate for preprocessing but can rise sharply for repeated motif scans and large permutation tests, so plan for at least **32–64 cores** and **256–512 GB RAM** on the main compute node. These are planning estimates, based on the public dataset sizes and the scale of the models being used. citeturn30view0turn10view0turn44view0turn38view0turn29search2turn29search21

For reproducibility, use a workflow manager such as **Snakemake or Nextflow**, package the environment in **Docker**, lock Python and R environments, and version datasets and intermediate tables with **DVC** or an equivalent artifact tracker. Keep one metadata manifest that records accession, version date, genome build, cell filtering choices, ortholog table version, and model checkpoint hash. In this project, reproducibility failures are more likely to come from silent annotation drift and accession updates than from the models themselves.

On ethical and legal constraints, treat human reproductive datasets conservatively. Some human uterine resources are fully open, while others may route through controlled-access archives such as EGA; the human endometrium mapping paper explicitly notes that some in vivo snRNA-seq data were deposited to EGA. Also check software and model licenses before standardizing the stack: AlphaGenome is available in preview via an API for non-commercial research, which may not be suitable for all deployment contexts. More generally, do not mix public human reproductive data with any re-identifiable metadata, and keep redistribution limited to the permissions of the source repositories and software licenses. citeturn46search4turn26search9turn27search9turn27search12

## Open questions and limitations

The main limitation is biological, not computational: in the sources reviewed, **public bat and spiny-mouse datasets do not yet mirror the richness of the human and mouse endometrial resources**, especially for cycle-staged chromatin and single-cell uterine profiling. That means the AI phase can produce a **high-quality shortlist of convergent regulators and elements**, but it cannot by itself close the case on convergence in the strict evolutionary sense without either broader outgroup inclusion or new focal-species data generation. citeturn14view1turn45search0turn20search0turn20search13turn24search1

The second limitation is model-domain mismatch. Geneformer is strongest in human single-cell settings; LINGER is strongest with paired multiome; and AlphaGenome, while highly promising, introduces access and reproducibility considerations. The safest way to manage that risk is to make all major conclusions depend on **agreement across at least one cell-state model, one GRN model, and one open sequence model** before a candidate advances to validation. citeturn29search21turn25search6turn26search4turn26search10turn27search0

The final implementation recommendation is therefore straightforward: **start with human + mouse + bat, use spiny mouse as a sequence-first species in Phase 1, and make the primary output a ranked, evidence-weighted candidate list rather than a claim of solved convergence**. That will give a skilled developer and computational biologist a tractable phase with a clear stopping point, and it will leave the project in the right shape for a follow-on phase that either adds new spiny-mouse uterine data or expands the phylogenetic test set. citeturn8search11turn36search11turn14view1turn20search0turn24search13
